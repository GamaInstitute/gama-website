## 1. Our mission and editorial principles {#introduction}

GAMA Institute is a nonprofit research, training and innovation initiative dedicated to making research in artificial intelligence and software engineering more accessible while developing the next generation of researchers. We welcome undergraduate and graduate students, doctoral researchers, early-career researchers, and anyone taking their first steps into the world of research. **No prior scientific publication experience is required.**

We value scientific accuracy, independent thinking, clear communication, openness, inclusion, and quality over quantity.

Every contribution should offer meaningful knowledge to its audience. Our goal is not simply to summarize scientific papers, promote products, or publish content for visibility. We want contributors to understand research, critically examine scientific findings, develop their own perspectives, and communicate knowledge that benefits the wider community.

Contributions published by GAMA Institute are educational and scientific communication outputs. They are not peer-reviewed scientific publications or endorsements of the underlying research.

### Why contribute to GAMA Institute? {#why-contribute}

Your research career does not have to begin with your first scientific publication. It can begin with your first meaningful contribution to the research community.

GAMA Institute was founded on a simple conviction: talented future researchers should not have to wait until they publish their first scientific paper to demonstrate their research interests, intellectual curiosity, and potential to the world. We aim to give aspiring researchers a distinctive opportunity to take their first steps into scientific research, develop essential research skills, and build a publicly accessible portfolio of their intellectual contributions.

Through GAMA Institute, you can:

- **Showcase your research interests internationally.** Demonstrate the scientific questions, technologies and research areas that genuinely interest you, even before publishing your first academic paper.
- **Build your scientific credibility.** Develop a portfolio of thoughtful, well-researched contributions that reflects your ability to understand, analyze and communicate scientific knowledge.
- **Develop essential research skills.** Learn to explore scientific literature, critically evaluate methodologies, interpret experimental findings, identify research limitations, and communicate complex ideas clearly.
- **Gain visibility within the research and innovation community.** Your contributions may reach researchers, university professors, research laboratory leaders, R&D professionals and other members of the international scientific community through our website and communication platforms.
- **Create opportunities for your future.** A strong contribution can demonstrate your potential to prospective research supervisors, collaborators, academic institutions and R&D employers. While opportunities are never guaranteed, your work can help others discover your abilities and interests.
- **Contribute regardless of your experience or English proficiency.** You do not need to be an established researcher, have published a scientific paper, or write perfect English. What matters most is your understanding, intellectual effort, scientific rigor and willingness to learn.

### Take this opportunity seriously {#take-it-seriously}

Your contribution is more than an article or a video. It is an early public demonstration of your potential as a researcher.

Every Research Summary or GAMA WeekPaper published under your name becomes part of your visible intellectual portfolio. It can demonstrate not only what you know, but also how you think, analyze evidence, question existing approaches, and communicate scientific ideas. Your work may be read or watched by people who could one day become your research supervisors, collaborators, colleagues or employers.

That is why we strongly encourage you to prioritize quality over quantity, develop your own analysis, verify your sources, and take ownership of every contribution you submit. You do not need to be an expert to begin. You need curiosity, discipline, intellectual honesty, and the willingness to do meaningful work.

We encourage contributors to publish multiple high-quality contributions over time, progressively building their expertise, research interests and scientific portfolios. At GAMA Institute, we believe that the next generation of researchers deserves the opportunity to be seen, heard and recognized long before their first scientific publication.

**Your research journey can start here.**

## 2. Scope and accepted contribution formats {#scope}

### Research areas and scope {#axes}

All submissions must align with at least one of GAMA Institute's five research axes:

- **Artificial Intelligence for Software Engineering (AI4SE):** applying AI techniques to improve software development, testing, debugging, maintenance and other software engineering activities.
- **Software Engineering for AI-Based Systems (SE4AI):** engineering reliable, maintainable, scalable and high-quality systems that incorporate artificial intelligence.
- **Cloud Computing and MLOps:** cloud-native technologies, DevOps, MLOps, LLMOps, infrastructure automation, and the deployment, monitoring and management of intelligent systems.
- **Cybersecurity:** software and AI security, vulnerability detection, secure development practices, threat analysis, and protection against emerging cyberattacks.
- **Responsible and Trustworthy AI:** AI fairness, transparency, explainability, privacy, accountability, safety, governance and auditing.

> **Not sure whether your topic fits?** Explore the [Research Summaries](/research-summaries), [GAMA WeekPaper presentations](/week-paper) and other contributions on our website to better understand our research interests and the types of contributions we welcome.

Topics that connect multiple research axes are particularly encouraged, provided they have a clear scientific foundation and offer meaningful insights to our community.

### A. Research Summary, written in English {#rs-format}

A Research Summary explains and critically discusses **at least one scientific research paper** in clear, accessible language. We strongly encourage contributions based on two or more papers, especially when they compare approaches, highlight similarities or differences, or provide new perspectives on a research topic.

A Research Summary can also be a **research-based technical tutorial** that implements, reproduces, adapts or demonstrates a technique, algorithm or methodology from at least one scientific paper.

#### What makes a good research-based tutorial?

- **Implement a research technique:** select an algorithm or methodology from a paper and explain how to implement and use it.
- **Simplify an existing implementation:** many papers provide replication packages. You do not need to reproduce them entirely. Instead, you can develop a simpler, better-documented and easier-to-understand implementation.
- **Implement a technique without existing code:** if the authors have not provided an implementation, you can develop your own based on the paper.
- **Compare multiple research approaches:** implement techniques from different papers to compare their behavior, advantages, limitations or results.

**Reproducibility is required.** Every tutorial must include the source code, instructions, dependencies and necessary resources so that GAMA reviewers can execute and verify the implementation.

> **Important:** we do not accept generic software tutorials. The tutorial must be directly grounded in scientific research and demonstrate a meaningful contribution from the cited paper(s). Simply mentioning or citing a research paper is not sufficient.

### B. GAMA WeekPaper, a recorded research presentation {#wp-format}

GAMA WeekPaper is a research video presentation that makes scientific findings accessible to a wider audience.

Presentations typically focus on one scientific paper, but contributors can explore multiple papers that share a common vision, propose contrasting methodologies, or offer complementary approaches.

We encourage creativity and innovative storytelling. Rather than simply presenting a paper section by section, make your video engaging, educational and scientifically accurate.

#### Language requirements

- **Spoken presentation:** French.
- **Slides (titles and content):** English.

This bilingual format allows you to present confidently in French while making your content accessible internationally. YouTube's translation and subtitle features can also help non-French-speaking audiences follow your presentation.

#### Recording requirements

- You must record the presentation yourself, using your own voice.
- You may choose to show your face or present using slides and voice narration only.
- AI-generated video presentations, synthetic narration and AI avatars are not accepted.

The goal is to demonstrate your understanding of the research while developing your scientific communication and presentation skills.

## 3. Finding research papers in computer science {#finding-papers}

A practical guide to [ACM Digital Library](https://dl.acm.org/) and [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/search/cs), for GAMA students and community contributors. Use it to find a paper for a Research Summary, a WeekPaper presentation, or an initial literature exploration. No paid search tool is required.

The examples focus on software engineering, cloud computing and MLOps, cybersecurity, and responsible and trustworthy AI. GitHub lists and the suggested X account provide additional discovery leads. ACM and arXiv are the two research libraries covered here, but the same approach applies to other academic platforms, including [Google Scholar](https://scholar.google.com/), [IEEE Xplore](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/Xplore/home.jsp) and [Engineering Village](https://www.engineeringvillage.com/app/search/quick/).

> **Important:** if you have institutional access through your university or organization, we encourage you to use the academic databases available to you. Otherwise, free platforms such as arXiv and Google Scholar are excellent starting points.

### 3.1 Build a focused search {#fp-requete}

Start with a question, such as "How can large language models generate unit tests?" Identify two main concepts, list alternative terms, and combine them. **Search in English**, even if you plan to present the paper in French.

| Tool | Purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| AND | Require both concepts | `unit test generation AND large language models` |
| OR | Include alternative terms | `unit testing OR test generation` |
| NOT | Exclude a term, with care | `software testing NOT hardware testing` |
| Quotation marks | Keep words together as a phrase | `"automated program repair"` |
| Parentheses | Group alternatives in ACM queries | `(unit testing OR test generation) AND large language models` |

Use uppercase Boolean operators and straight quotation marks.

#### Broaden or narrow deliberately

**Too few results:** remove one concept, shorten an exact phrase, or try synonyms. **Too many results:** add a specific task or language, search titles or abstracts, or apply a date filter. Use NOT sparingly, because it can remove relevant studies. Adding `survey` can help you find background reading.

### 3.2 Query examples by research area {#fp-exemples}

These are starting expressions, not guaranteed result sets. Use them directly in ACM's Boolean search and in arXiv search.

#### Software engineering

```
unit test generation AND large language models
automated program repair AND test generation
code generation AND software quality
requirements engineering AND traceability
code review AND defect detection
```

#### Cloud computing and MLOps

```
infrastructure as code AND large language models
MLOps AND monitoring
cloud computing AND resource allocation
```

#### Cybersecurity

```
prompt injection AND large language models
vulnerability detection AND deep learning
software supply chain AND security
```

#### Responsible and trustworthy AI

```
machine learning AND fairness
AI governance AND software engineering
large language models AND cultural bias
```

### 3.3 Search arXiv computer science {#fp-arxiv}

[Open arXiv computer science search](https://arxiv.org/search/cs). arXiv provides free manuscripts, including preprints and author versions of published papers. **arXiv does not peer-review submissions.**

1. Open arXiv.
2. Run your query.
3. Adjust the search parameters (field searched, sort order).
4. Open promising papers, read their abstracts, and use the available PDF or HTML full text.

![arXiv results page for the unit test generation query](/publication-guidelines/arxiv-search.webp "arXiv results for the unit test generation query. The “All fields” menu (red box) narrows the search to titles or abstracts; the [pdf] link (arrow) opens the paper."){1839x855}

#### Check that an arXiv paper was peer-reviewed

arXiv gives free access to scientific papers, but not all papers on arXiv are peer-reviewed. Before selecting one for your GAMA contribution:

1. Find a paper on arXiv that interests you.
2. Copy its exact title and search for it on [Google Scholar](https://scholar.google.com/), [ACM Digital Library](https://dl.acm.org/), [IEEE Xplore](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/) or another academic database.
3. Verify its publication status. If the paper has been published in a peer-reviewed journal or conference, you can select it for your contribution.
4. If you cannot verify a peer-reviewed version, choose another paper and repeat the process.

> **Example:** suppose you find the paper "Beyond Leaderboards: A Method for Application-Specific Capability Profiling of Large Language Models" on arXiv. Searching its exact title in major academic databases does not reveal a verified peer-reviewed publication. You should therefore treat it as a preprint unless further publication evidence is found, and select another paper under GAMA's peer-review requirement.

#### You can also reverse the process

If you discover an interesting peer-reviewed paper in ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore or another academic database but cannot access its full text:

1. Copy the paper's title.
2. Search for it on arXiv or Google Scholar.
3. If a free version is available, use it to study the research, checking whether it differs from the published version.

> **Remember:** finding a paper in an academic database does not automatically mean it has been peer-reviewed. Always verify its publication venue and status.

### 3.4 Search ACM Digital Library {#fp-acm}

[Open ACM Digital Library](https://dl.acm.org/). ACM provides computing journals and conference proceedings. Its move to full open access began in January 2026. The broader ACM Guide to Computing Literature also indexes other publishers; an indexed external paper is not necessarily available in full text through ACM.

1. Open ACM Digital Library.
2. Run your query.
3. Adjust the filters (publication date, people, publications).
4. Open promising papers, read their abstracts, and use the available PDF or HTML full text.

#### Worked example: unit test generation

Question: how are large language models used to generate unit tests? Start with:

```
unit test generation AND large language models
```

If this is too restrictive, try the following ACM expression to include alternative wording:

```
(unit test generation OR unit testing) AND (large language models OR LLM)
```

Compare the results before applying a recent date range. Older papers can supply important foundations; recent papers reveal newer methods and evaluations. A search match is a candidate, not evidence that the study answers your question.

![ACM Digital Library results page for the unit test generation query](/publication-guidelines/acm-search.webp "ACM results for the query above. Filters are in the left column (red box); the PDF icon (arrow) opens the full text."){1825x862}

### 3.5 Use curated collections as starting points {#fp-collections}

GitHub reading lists help you learn terminology, identify foundational papers, and find code or datasets. Their coverage and maintenance vary: some entries may be old and some links broken. They are not complete or necessarily current literature reviews, and a recently edited repository does not mean every topic has been updated.

- **[Software engineering for AI and ML](https://github.com/ckaestne/seaibib)**: an annotated bibliography with explanatory context. Start here to understand how software engineering applies to systems with AI or ML components.
- **[Awesome Software Engineering for Machine Learning](https://github.com/SE-ML/awesome-seml)**: a widely followed collection covering development practices, testing, quality, deployment and operation, with research and practitioner material.
- **[Awesome MLOps](https://github.com/visenger/awesome-mlops)**: references on operating ML systems, including a dedicated papers collection. Separate research papers from tools, talks and tutorials.
- **[Awesome LLM Security](https://github.com/corca-ai/awesome-llm-security)**: papers, benchmarks and tools on LLM attacks and defences. Follow the original paper links to verify claims and publication status.
- **[Awesome Responsible AI](https://github.com/AthenaCore/AwesomeResponsibleAI)**: a broad collection of research and other resources on responsible and trustworthy AI. Distinguish academic evidence from reports and guidance.
- **[Awesome LLM as a Judge](https://github.com/llm-as-a-judge/Awesome-LLM-as-a-judge)**: research papers on using large language models to evaluate AI-generated outputs: evaluation methods, applications, and challenges involving reliability, bias and code correctness.
- **[Awesome Machine Learning for Software Engineering](https://github.com/saltudelft/ml4se)**: research papers, theses, datasets and tools on applying machine learning to software engineering, such as code generation, completion, summarization and code changes.
- **[Awesome LLM for Software Engineering](https://github.com/iSEngLab/AwesomeLLM4SE)**: a research collection accompanying a survey of large language models for software engineering, organized around software engineering tasks and code language models.
- **[Awesome Agent for Software Engineering](https://github.com/DeepSoftwareAnalytics/Awesome-Agent4SE)**: a collection accompanying research on LLM-based agents for software engineering, and how agents support software development.

#### Find newer research from an older list

Choose one useful entry and note its title, year, authors and keywords. Find it in ACM or arXiv. Search those keywords again with a more recent date range. Follow references for foundations and, where available, citing articles for later work. Keep both the foundational paper and relevant updates.

#### Follow the suggested X account

[ComputerPapers on X](https://x.com/ComputerPapers). Use it as an additional discovery lead.

## 4. Research Summary editorial standards {#research-summaries}

| | |
|---|---|
| Purpose | Explain a research paper in clear, accessible language so that readers, including people without a technical background, can understand its main ideas and relevance. |
| Suggested length | 500 to 900 words, excluding references. |
| Audience | Curious readers, students and practitioners. No specialist knowledge should be assumed. |
| Language | English. |

Start with an informative and engaging **title**, then your name ("Summary by") and the original paper (title, authors and link).

**Contributor identification:** submit your Research Summary with a brief author bio, a profile photo, and a link to your LinkedIn profile, if available. These details accompany the published summary to identify and credit its author(s).

### 4.1 Recommended structure for a Research Summary {#rs-structure}

#### Table of contents (mandatory)

Every Research Summary must include a table of contents, placed immediately before your main content. It should list the main sections and subsections to help readers navigate your contribution.

#### 1. Overview

_What is this research about?_

Introduce the topic, the problem being addressed, and what the researchers wanted to understand or improve. Briefly explain how they approached it, without unnecessary technical detail. **One or two short paragraphs.**

#### 2. Why it matters

_Why should readers care?_

Explain why the problem is important and who or what might be affected. Connect it to a familiar situation in software, technology, education, work or everyday life. **One or two short paragraphs.** A simple example can help.

#### 3. Key findings or ideas

_What should readers remember?_

Present two or three main findings, contributions or arguments. For each, explain the idea and what it means in plain language. Include important numbers when useful, with enough context to understand them.

You can add an original image, diagram or table to make the ideas easier to understand (see [Images and tables](#rs-visuels)).

#### 4. Discussion and implications

_What can we learn from this research?_

Discuss what the findings could mean for people who develop, use or are affected by technology. Mention an important limitation or unanswered question that helps readers interpret the research fairly. You can use these prompts:

- How might these ideas influence practice or everyday decisions?
- In which situations might the findings be useful?
- What should readers be cautious about?
- What is your own interpretation, and how does it relate to the paper?

Choose the most relevant prompts and write **one or two short paragraphs.** Clearly distinguish your interpretation from the authors' findings.

#### 5. Moving forward

_What comes next?_

Briefly explain what could be explored or improved next. End with a clear takeaway or an open question that invites readers to reflect. **One short paragraph.**

#### References

Link to the original paper(s) and any other sources you used.

### 4.2 Recommended structure for a research-based tutorial {#rs-tutorial}

#### Table of contents (mandatory)

For research-based technical tutorials, we encourage a clear, practical structure inspired by educational platforms such as [freeCodeCamp](https://www.freecodecamp.org/news/how-to-detect-financial-fraud-patterns-with-knowledge-graphs-a-production-guide/). For example, a tutorial's table of contents could include:

- **Introduction and research background:** identify the exact source paper(s), the proposed technique or methodology, and what the tutorial implements or adapts.
- **Prerequisites and environment setup:** specify the audience, knowledge, software versions, dependencies, hardware and data requirements.
- **Understanding the research technique:** describe the relevant research idea faithfully, including assumptions and any departure from the paper.
- **Step-by-step implementation:** provide runnable, readable code, configurations, commands and explanations; include a repository link when useful.
- **Running and testing the implementation:** show how to run or test the implementation, expected outputs, evaluation criteria and reproducibility limitations.
- **Results, limitations and lessons learned:** distinguish the original paper's results from your own observations; address limitations, ethics, security and appropriate use.
- **Conclusion and references.**

The structure is flexible. Adapt it to your topic, but make sure readers can understand the research, follow your implementation, and reproduce the results.

### 4.3 Simple writing rules {#rs-regles}

- **Understand before summarizing.** Read beyond the abstract. Focus on the paper's purpose, main ideas, results and limitations.
- **Use your own words.** Explain the ideas as you would to a curious classmate.
- **Keep the language accessible.** Define unfamiliar terms and abbreviations. Use short paragraphs and concrete examples.
- **Stay faithful to the paper.** Do not exaggerate findings or present possibilities as established facts.
- **Choose what matters most.** You do not need to explain every experiment, equation or section.
- **Separate findings from opinions.** Use wording such as "The authors found…" and "This could suggest…" to make the distinction clear.
- **Check your sources.** Verify names, numbers, claims and links. Check any AI-assisted content against the original paper.

### 4.4 Using images and tables {#rs-visuels}

Visuals are optional. Include them when they help explain an idea.

- **Create your own diagram or illustration** to explain a concept, process or practical example.
- **Build your own table** to summarize findings, compare approaches or organize key ideas.
- Add a short caption explaining the visual.
- Label invented examples as _illustrative_. Preserve the meaning and context of any results you present.
- If you reuse or adapt an existing visual, credit its source and check its reuse conditions.

> **Before submitting:** can someone unfamiliar with the topic understand what the research explored, what it contributed, and why it matters?

Submit your Research Summary through the [form on the Research Summaries page](/research-summaries#contribute).

## 5. GAMA WeekPaper production standards {#weekpaper}

Download the [GAMA PowerPoint template](download:slide_template) and use it to prepare your slides. Follow its visual identity while adapting layouts, diagrams and tables to explain your paper clearly.

### Follow the GAMA presentation structure or suggest an innovative one {#wp-structure}

Organize your presentation around the following sections:

| Section | What to include |
|---|---|
| Intro | Introduce a realistic situation or engaging question. Present the paper and explain why the problem matters. |
| Key concepts | Explain the essential terms and ideas the audience needs to understand the research. |
| Methodology | Describe what the researchers did and how they evaluated their approach, without unnecessary technical detail. |
| Results | Present the most important findings and explain what they mean. |
| Discussion & implications | Discuss practical lessons, important limitations and possible future directions. Distinguish your interpretation from the authors' conclusions. |
| Question to the audience | End with an accessible, open question that encourages viewers to share their experiences or perspectives. |

Submit your presentation through the [form on the WeekPaper page](/week-paper#contribute).

## 6. Responsible use of generative AI {#ai}

The GAMA Institute Policy on AI Use applies in full to every Research Summary, research-based tutorial and WeekPaper. AI may support your learning and communication, but **the understanding, reasoning and final contribution must be your own.**

### 6.1 Acceptable uses of AI {#ai-acceptable}

You may use AI tools to:

- Explore a topic and identify keywords or concepts to investigate.
- Obtain preliminary explanations of unfamiliar terminology.
- Brainstorm questions, examples or possible structures.
- Improve grammar, spelling, clarity or translation.
- Receive feedback on a draft you have written.
- Assist with designing illustrations, diagrams or slides, provided you verify their accuracy and disclose their use.

AI-generated explanations and references must be checked against the original paper and other reliable sources. Exploring a paper through AI does not replace reading it.

### 6.2 Your own intellectual contribution {#ai-own-work}

The submitted work must reflect your understanding of the paper, your selection of important ideas, and your interpretation of its implications. You must personally:

- Read the original paper beyond its abstract.
- Understand the problem, approach, main findings and important limitations.
- Decide what the audience needs to learn.
- Develop the explanation and discussion in your own words.
- Verify claims, numerical results, references and visual representations.
- Be able to explain and defend the content if a reviewer asks questions.

Your own contribution should be evident in the clarity of your explanations, the examples you choose, and the connections you make. Polished language alone does not demonstrate understanding.

GAMA welcomes contributors who are still developing their English writing or French presentation skills. Clear, thoughtful work is more valuable than elaborate wording.

### 6.3 Prohibited uses {#ai-prohibited}

You must not:

- Submit a summary, analysis or presentation produced entirely or substantially by AI as your own work.
- Ask AI to generate the complete contribution and make only superficial changes before submitting it.
- Present AI-generated interpretations as conclusions established by the paper.
- Include fabricated references, results, quotations, experiments or claims.
- Use AI to conceal plagiarism or misrepresent authorship.

**AI-generated video presentations are not accepted.** GAMA WeekPaper must be presented by the contributor, using their own voice and explanation. Synthetic narration, voice cloning, AI avatars and fully generated presenters are prohibited.

You may present with or without showing your face. Standard recording and editing tools, including noise reduction, captioning and trimming, are permitted when they preserve your actual presentation. Contributors who need an accessibility accommodation should [contact GAMA](mailto:contact@gamainstitute.ca) before recording.

### 6.4 Disclosure of AI assistance {#ai-disclosure}

Include a brief statement with every submission describing whether and how you used AI.

> **Example:** "I used [tool name] to explore unfamiliar concepts and improve English grammar. I read the original paper, developed the analysis, and verified all claims and references myself."
>
> If no AI tools were used, state: "No generative AI tools were used in preparing this contribution."

Disclosing AI assistance does not make prohibited use acceptable. Contributors remain responsible for the entire submission.

## 7. Review and clarification {#review}

GAMA reviewers assess scientific accuracy, understanding, originality of explanation, and compliance with these guidelines.

If a submission appears to rely excessively on AI, GAMA may pause publication and ask the contributor to explain selected claims, discuss the paper, provide preparation notes, or revise the work.

A reviewer's impression may trigger clarification, but it does not by itself establish a violation. AI-detection scores, polished language or writing style alone are insufficient grounds for a finding. GAMA will consider the content, the contributor's explanation and other relevant evidence before reaching a decision.

## 8. Warnings and consequences {#consequences}

GAMA applies the following process when a violation is established.

### First confirmed violation {#first-violation}

GAMA will send one confidential written warning explaining the concern and the applicable rule. The affected contribution may be declined or returned for substantial revision. The contributor may respond and demonstrate their own understanding before a final publication decision.

### Second confirmed violation after the warning {#second-violation}

GAMA will decline the affected contribution, stop accepting future contributions from that person, and remove their address from GAMA's contributor mailing list.

If the person is also subscribed to GAMA's general newsletter, GAMA will explain any resulting removal in the private decision notice. Any removal will be handled consistently with GAMA's [Privacy Policy](/privacy), retaining only the minimal restricted record necessary to administer the decision.

Before imposing these consequences, GAMA will give the contributor an opportunity to respond. A contributor may request reconsideration if relevant evidence was overlooked or a factual error affected the decision.

## 9. Confidential handling {#confidentiality}

Concerns, warnings, responses and decisions will be handled confidentially and shared only with people who need the information to review or administer the matter.

GAMA will not publicly name, shame or announce sanctions against a contributor. Relevant records will be protected and retained according to GAMA's privacy and retention practices, subject to applicable legal obligations.

## 10. Build your research practice {#practice}

GAMA encourages you to submit multiple contributions over time. Each contribution should demonstrate your own learning, careful reading and thoughtful communication.

These publicly shared contributions can help you develop a research portfolio and show the scientific community what interests you. They are learning and research-communication activities; publication by GAMA does not make them peer-reviewed research articles.

**Use AI to strengthen your skills. Take ownership of the work that carries your name.**

## 11. Final contributor checklist {#checklist}

- [ ] My topic fits a GAMA research axis and is based on at least one original scientific paper.
- [ ] I read and verified the original paper(s), their evidence and their limitations.
- [ ] My Research Summary is in English and contains a table of contents; multi-paper comparisons are meaningful where used.
- [ ] If I wrote a tutorial, it demonstrably implements or adapts an identifiable research technique and the code is tested.
- [ ] If I prepared a WeekPaper, I speak French, use English slides, and provide my own authentic recording.
- [ ] I cite and license source material appropriately and distinguish research findings from my own interpretations.
- [ ] I included the contributor bio and profile materials, all required submission assets, and an AI-use disclosure.
- [ ] I proofread the contribution and can explain and defend it to an editor.

A question about these guidelines? [Write to us](mailto:contact@gamainstitute.ca).

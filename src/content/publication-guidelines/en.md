## Introduction {#introduction}

GAMA Institute makes research accessible to as many people as possible. Students, early-career researchers and community members can contribute in two ways:

- **[Research Summary](#research-summaries)**: a 500 to 900-word written summary that explains a research paper in clear language.
- **[WeekPaper](#weekpaper)**: a video presentation of a paper, in French, with slides in English.

### Our five research axes {#axes}

The paper you choose must relate to one of our five research axes:

- Artificial intelligence and machine learning
- Software engineering
- Cloud computing
- Cybersecurity
- Responsible and trustworthy AI

You do not need to be an expert: you only need to understand the paper well enough to explain its main ideas accurately. First-time contributors are welcome. No paper in mind yet? See [Finding a paper](#finding-papers).

## Writing a Research Summary {#research-summaries}

A Research Summary explains a research paper in clear, accessible language, so that readers, including people without a technical background, can understand its main ideas and why they matter.

### At a glance {#rs-en-bref}

| | |
|---|---|
| Purpose | Explain a research paper so that readers without a technical background can understand its main ideas and relevance. |
| Length | 500 to 900 words, excluding references. |
| Audience | Curious readers, students and practitioners. Assume no specialist knowledge. |
| Language | All research summaries are written in **English**, including those by native French speakers. Simple wording is encouraged over complex academic language. |

Still looking for a paper? Start with [Finding a paper](#finding-papers).

### The 5-part structure {#rs-structure}

Start with an informative, engaging **title**, then give your name and the original paper's reference (title, authors, year and link).

#### 1. Overview

_What is this research about?_

Introduce the topic, the problem being addressed, and what the researchers wanted to understand or improve. Briefly explain how they approached it, without unnecessary technical detail. **One or two short paragraphs.**

#### 2. Why it matters

_Why should readers care?_

Explain why the problem is important and who or what might be affected. Connect it to a familiar situation in software, technology, education, work or everyday life. **One or two short paragraphs.** A simple example can help.

#### 3. Key findings or ideas

_What should readers remember?_

Present two or three main findings, contributions or arguments. For each, explain the idea and what it means in plain language. Include important numbers when useful, with enough context to understand them.

You can add an original image, diagram or table to make the ideas easier to understand (see [Images and tables](#rs-visuels)).

#### 4. Discussion and implications

_What can we learn from this research?_

Discuss what the findings could mean for people who develop, use or are affected by technology. Mention an important limitation or unanswered question that helps readers interpret the research fairly. Pick the most relevant of these prompts:

- How might these ideas influence practice or everyday decisions?
- In which situations might the findings be useful?
- What should readers be cautious about?
- What is your own interpretation, and how does it relate to the paper?

**One or two short paragraphs.** Clearly distinguish your interpretation from the authors' findings.

#### 5. Moving forward

_What comes next?_

Briefly explain what could be explored or improved next. End with a clear takeaway or an open question that invites readers to reflect. **One short paragraph.**

#### References

Link to the original paper and any additional sources you used.

### Simple writing rules {#rs-regles}

- **Understand before summarizing.** Read beyond the abstract. Focus on the paper's purpose, main ideas, results and limitations.
- **Use your own words.** Explain the ideas as you would to a curious classmate.
- **Keep the language accessible.** Define unfamiliar terms and abbreviations. Use short paragraphs and concrete examples.
- **Stay faithful to the paper.** Do not exaggerate findings or present possibilities as established facts.
- **Choose what matters most.** You do not need to explain every experiment, equation or section.
- **Separate findings from opinions.** Use wording such as "The authors found…" and "This could suggest…" to make the distinction clear.
- **Check your sources.** Verify names, numbers, claims and links. Check any AI-assisted content against the original paper.

### Images and tables {#rs-visuels}

Visuals are optional. Include them when they help explain an idea.

- **Create your own diagram or illustration** to explain a concept, process or practical example.
- **Build your own table** to summarize findings, compare approaches or organize key ideas.
- Add a short caption explaining the visual.
- Label invented examples as _illustrative_. Preserve the meaning and context of any results you present.
- If you reuse or adapt an existing visual, credit its source and check its reuse conditions.

### Submitting your summary {#rs-soumettre}

Send your summary (Word or PDF) through the [form on the Research Summaries page](/research-summaries#contribute). The form also asks for a short bio, a profile photo and, if you have one, your LinkedIn profile. These accompany the published summary to identify and credit you.

The GAMA team reviews every summary with you before publication (see [Review and publication](#review)).

> **Before submitting:** can someone unfamiliar with the topic understand what the research explored, what it contributed, and why it matters?

## Presenting a paper on WeekPaper {#weekpaper}

GAMA WeekPaper invites French-speaking students and community members to present, on video, a paper related to one of our [five research axes](#axes). Our goal is to make research accessible while helping contributors develop their understanding, presentation skills and confidence.

### 1. Choose a research paper {#wp-article}

Select a paper that interests you and offers useful insights for the GAMA community.

- Note its title, authors and link.
- Focus on one main paper, using additional sources when helpful.

No paper in mind yet? [Finding a paper](#finding-papers) explains how to search ACM Digital Library and arXiv.

> If you have difficulty accessing or downloading the paper, [write to us](mailto:contact@gamainstitute.ca) and we will help.

### 2. Download the presentation template {#wp-modele}

Download the [GAMA PowerPoint template](download:slide_template) and use it to prepare your slides. Follow its visual identity while adapting layouts, diagrams and tables to explain your paper clearly.

### 3. Follow the GAMA structure {#wp-structure}

Organize your presentation around the following sections. You may also suggest an innovative structure, as long as it explains the research clearly.

| Section | What to include |
|---|---|
| Intro | Introduce a realistic situation or engaging question. Present the paper and explain why the problem matters. |
| Key concepts | Explain the essential terms and ideas the audience needs to understand the research. |
| Methodology | Describe what the researchers did and how they evaluated their approach, without unnecessary technical detail. |
| Results | Present the most important findings and explain what they mean. |
| Discussion & implications | Discuss practical lessons, important limitations and possible future directions. Distinguish your interpretation from the authors' conclusions. |
| Question to the audience | End with an accessible, open question that encourages viewers to share their experiences or perspectives. |

### 4. Language and slide design {#wp-langue}

The presentation is intended for a **French-speaking audience**:

- Deliver your presentation **in French**.
- Write slide titles and content **in English**, including if you are a native French speaker.
- Explain technical terms and abbreviations in accessible language.
- Keep slides concise, with one main message per slide.

You may create your own images, diagrams or tables to explain concepts or summarize findings. Add source credits and clearly label illustrative examples. Credit any reused or adapted visuals and check their reuse conditions.

### 5. Record your presentation {#wp-enregistrer}

Record a video of your presentation. You may follow the style of [GAMA WeekPaper videos](/week-paper) or choose another format that communicates the research clearly.

**You may record with or without showing your face.** For example, record your slides with voice narration, appear alongside your slides, or combine these formats.

Make sure your voice is clear, your slides are readable and the recording is easy to follow.

### 6. Prepare the publication materials {#wp-publication}

Along with your presentation, prepare:

- **A proposed YouTube title:** clear, engaging and faithful to the research.
- **A YouTube description in French:** introduce the topic, explain what viewers will learn, and include the paper reference and link.
- **A profile photo:** it identifies you in the video thumbnail, including when your face does not appear in the recording.
- **A brief bio:** a few sentences introducing your background and interests.
- **Your LinkedIn profile link, if you have one:** to credit you and help viewers discover your work.

Browse the existing videos on the [GAMA Institute YouTube channel](https://www.youtube.com/@gamainstitut) for examples of titles and descriptions.

### 7. Choose your publication preference {#wp-diffusion}

When submitting, select one option:

- **YouTube and LinkedIn:** GAMA publishes the video on its YouTube channel and shares or promotes your contribution on its LinkedIn page.
- **YouTube only:** GAMA publishes the video on its YouTube channel, without sharing or promoting it on LinkedIn.

### 8. Submit your contribution {#wp-soumettre}

Use the [submission form on the WeekPaper page](/week-paper#contribute). It asks for:

- a link to your presentation video (Google Drive, OneDrive, Dropbox or WeTransfer);
- your editable PowerPoint slides;
- the original paper's reference and link;
- your proposed YouTube title and description;
- your full name, profile photo, brief bio and, if you have one, your LinkedIn profile link;
- your publication preference.

> **Before submitting:** can a French-speaking viewer unfamiliar with the paper understand its problem, approach, main findings and practical significance?

## Review and publication {#review}

Explain the research in your own words, verify numerical results, acknowledge important limitations and credit your sources.

The GAMA team reviews every contribution for clarity, scientific accuracy, accessibility and alignment with these guidelines. We may ask for changes before publication; any substantive change is submitted to you.

Published contributions credit their authors or presenters and acknowledge the original research authors. You keep ownership of your contribution, which is released under a CC BY 4.0 licence: see our [Open Access Policy](/open-access) and [Terms of Use](/terms#contributions).

A question about these guidelines? [Write to us](mailto:contact@gamainstitute.ca).

## Finding a paper {#finding-papers}

A practical guide to [ACM Digital Library](https://dl.acm.org/) and [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/search/cs), the two research libraries covered here. Use it to find a paper to summarize or present. No paid search tool is required. The examples follow our five research axes; GitHub lists and an X account provide additional leads.

### 1. Build a focused search {#fp-requete}

Start with a question, such as "How can large language models generate unit tests?" Identify two main concepts, list alternative terms, and combine them. **Search in English**, even if you plan to present the paper in French.

| Tool | Purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| AND | Require both concepts | `unit test generation AND large language models` |
| OR | Include alternative terms | `unit testing OR test generation` |
| NOT | Exclude a term, with care | `software testing NOT hardware testing` |
| Quotation marks | Keep words together as a phrase | `"automated program repair"` |
| Parentheses | Group alternatives (ACM) | `(unit testing OR test generation) AND large language models` |

Write Boolean operators in uppercase and use straight quotation marks.

#### Broaden or narrow deliberately

**Too few results:** remove one concept, shorten an exact phrase, or try synonyms. **Too many results:** add a specific task or language, search titles or abstracts, or apply a date filter.

Use NOT sparingly, because it can remove relevant studies. Adding `survey` to your query helps you find background reading.

### 2. Search ACM Digital Library {#fp-acm}

[ACM Digital Library](https://dl.acm.org/) provides computing journals and conference proceedings. Its move to full open access began in January 2026. The broader ACM Guide to Computing Literature also indexes other publishers, so an indexed external paper is not necessarily available in full text through ACM.

1. Open [ACM Digital Library](https://dl.acm.org/).
2. Run your query.
3. Adjust the filters (publication date, authors, venues).
4. Open promising papers, read their abstracts, and use the PDF or HTML full text.

#### Worked example: unit test generation

Question: how are large language models used to generate unit tests? Start with:

```
unit test generation AND large language models
```

If this is too restrictive, use parentheses to include alternative wording:

```
(unit test generation OR unit testing) AND (large language models OR LLM)
```

Compare the results before applying a recent date range. Older papers often supply important foundations; recent papers reveal newer methods and evaluations. A search match is a candidate, not evidence that the study answers your question.

![ACM Digital Library results page for the unit test generation query](/publication-guidelines/acm-search.png "ACM results for the query above. Filters are in the left column (red box); the PDF icon (arrow) opens the full text.")

### 3. Search arXiv {#fp-arxiv}

[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/search/cs) provides free manuscripts, including preprints and author versions of published papers. **arXiv does not peer-review submissions.**

1. Open the [arXiv computer science search](https://arxiv.org/search/cs).
2. Run your query.
3. Pick the field to search (title, abstract or all fields) and the sort order.
4. Open promising papers, read their abstracts, and use the PDF or HTML full text.

![arXiv results page for the same query](/publication-guidelines/arxiv-search.png "arXiv results for the same query. The “All fields” menu (red box) narrows the search to titles or abstracts; the [pdf] link (arrow) opens the paper.")

### 4. Select and record a paper {#fp-selection}

Read the title and abstract, then scan the introduction, main results and limitations. Can you explain the problem and contribution? Check what data, systems or participants support the conclusions. Select a manageable paper whose evidence you can discuss accurately.

Record the paper title, authors, year, an accessible link, and a one-sentence reason for selecting it.

> If access fails, search the exact title in the other library. If the paper is still unavailable, [write to us](mailto:contact@gamainstitute.ca) with its title and link: we can help find an accessible version or a suitable alternative.

### 5. Query examples by research axis {#fp-exemples}

These are starting points, not guaranteed result sets. Use them as-is in ACM's Boolean search. In arXiv, combine the terms with the advanced search.

#### Software engineering

```
unit test generation AND large language models
automated program repair AND test generation
code generation AND software quality
requirements engineering AND traceability
code review AND defect detection
```

#### Cloud computing and MLOps

```
infrastructure as code AND large language models
MLOps AND monitoring
cloud computing AND resource allocation
```

#### Cybersecurity

```
prompt injection AND large language models
vulnerability detection AND deep learning
software supply chain AND security
```

#### Responsible and trustworthy AI

```
machine learning AND fairness
AI governance AND software engineering
large language models AND cultural bias
```

### 6. Start from curated collections {#fp-collections}

GitHub reading lists help you learn terminology, identify foundational papers, and find code or datasets. Their coverage and maintenance vary: some entries may be old and some links broken. They are not complete or necessarily current literature reviews.

- **[Software engineering for AI and ML](https://github.com/ckaestne/seaibib)**: an annotated bibliography. Start here to understand how software engineering applies to systems with AI components.
- **[Awesome Software Engineering for Machine Learning](https://github.com/SE-ML/awesome-seml)**: development practices, testing, quality, deployment and operation, with research and practitioner material.
- **[Awesome MLOps](https://github.com/visenger/awesome-mlops)**: operating ML systems, including a dedicated papers collection. Separate research papers from tools, talks and tutorials.
- **[Awesome LLM Security](https://github.com/corca-ai/awesome-llm-security)**: papers, benchmarks and tools on LLM attacks and defences. Follow the original paper links to verify claims.
- **[Awesome Responsible AI](https://github.com/AthenaCore/AwesomeResponsibleAI)**: research and resources on responsible and trustworthy AI. Distinguish academic evidence from reports and guidance.
- **[Awesome LLM as a Judge](https://github.com/llm-as-a-judge/Awesome-LLM-as-a-judge)**: using LLMs to evaluate AI-generated outputs, and the challenges of reliability, bias and code correctness.
- **[Awesome Machine Learning for Software Engineering](https://github.com/saltudelft/ml4se)**: papers, theses, datasets and tools on applying machine learning to software engineering (code generation, completion, summarization and changes).
- **[Awesome LLM for Software Engineering](https://github.com/iSEngLab/AwesomeLLM4SE)**: the papers from a survey of LLMs for software engineering, organized by task.
- **[Awesome Agent for Software Engineering](https://github.com/DeepSoftwareAnalytics/Awesome-Agent4SE)**: LLM-based agents for software development.

#### Find newer research from an older list

Choose one useful entry and note its title, year, authors and keywords. Find it in ACM or arXiv, then search those keywords again over a more recent date range. Follow its references for foundations and, where available, the papers that cite it for later work.

#### Follow an X account

[ComputerPapers on X](https://x.com/ComputerPapers) regularly flags new computer science papers.

### 7. Checklist before you contribute {#fp-verifications}

- [ ] I can explain the paper's problem and main contribution.
- [ ] I have read the original paper, not just a post or a summary.
- [ ] I have checked key results, comparisons and limitations.
- [ ] I have saved the paper title and an accessible link.
